How hard are the hardest benchmarks?

You installed your agent in Week 0 and have worked through this week's courses. In the session you point it at someone else's hardest problems. First look at the latest numbers on the hardest public software-engineering benchmarks, then open real tasks from them. In pairs, take ten minutes per task: use your coding agent to understand the task and its size, then estimate how long it would take, how complex it is and how well specified it is. Only then do you see the published answers.

Where the frontier stands

We read every board on 9 October 2026. The hardest public boards show saturation followed by replacement. SWE-bench Verified last set a record on its own board in December 2025, at 79.2%. OpenAI stopped reporting it in February 2026, after finding that 59.4% of 138 audited hard tasks had flawed tests or descriptions and that every frontier model it probed could reproduce gold patches or problem text for some tasks. OpenAI recommended SWE-Bench Pro instead. Scale's public board tops out at 61.5% (Muse Spark 1.1, July 2026), yet in July OpenAI estimated that about 30% of Pro's public tasks are broken and withdrew its recommendation. Scale released a revised 642-task V2 on 22 September.

Terminal-Bench now versions itself continuously: among the models its team compared, the best score was 83.8% on version 2.1 and 34.4% on 3.0, and the 4.0 board is led by Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code at 64.8%. FrontierCode, Cognition's private benchmark graded on whether a maintainer would merge the patch, is led by Claude Opus 5.5 at 54.6. METR's 50% time horizon reached about 17 hours for Claude Mythos Preview, beyond the 16 hours METR calls reliable, and METR stopped updating the page in September. Passing tests also overstates usefulness: maintainers would not merge about half of test-passing Verified patches.

The Week 1 session, minute by minute

    Rules for the agent

    The agent may read and run code. It must not edit files, search the web, or open README.md, task.toml, solution/, tests/ or the linked pull request: those hold the answer or the published time. Give it this starter prompt, identical for Claude Code and Codex, then read its answer and probe it with follow-up questions.

    Help us scope this task before anyone writes code. Read TASK.md (or instruction.md). Do not edit any file, do not search the web, and do not open README.md, task.toml, solution/, tests/ or any linked pull request. Then (1) list the files and functions involved, one line each; (2) describe what a complete solution must change; (3) name the two riskiest unknowns; (4) say how you would check the solution works. Keep the answer under 300 words.

    Every estimate answers one question. It asks about an experienced engineer rather than about you, because the published human times assume roughly that person.

    How many minutes would an experienced engineer, new to this repository, need without AI help to reach a solution that passes the task's tests?

    The six tasks

    Two tasks from each of SWE-bench Verified, Terminal-Bench 4.0 and SWE-Bench Pro V2. Each pair scopes two of them, one per round. The cards hold back the published answers until you reveal them, because the upstream folders and IDs give them away.

    Pair p takes task ((p − 1) mod 6) + 1 in round 1 and task ((p + 2) mod 6) + 1 in round 2, so each pair meets two benchmarks.

    Your estimates against the ground truth

    Each revealed task with a published human time gets a row on a log scale, because the times run from under 15 minutes to 960. Terminal-Bench tasks compare with the task author's point estimate as log2(estimate ÷ point): +1 is twice as long, −1 half as long. SWE-bench Verified tasks count as inside the annotators' bucket, or else take the log2 distance to the nearer bound.

    SWE-Bench Pro publishes no per-task human time, so Tasks 5 and 6 never appear here. This page shows your own estimates only; the room's spread comes from the files the facilitator gathers.

    Exit question

    Which of your three numbers (time, complexity, specification quality) best predicted whether the agents solved your two tasks, and what does that suggest about how you will choose work for an agent this term?

    Why we estimate before we look

    Prediction before the reveal carries most of the learning. Crouch and colleagues found that students who only watched a physics demonstration understood the underlying concepts no better than students who never saw it, while students who predicted the outcome first showed significantly greater understanding [1]. Asking questions before instruction has a related benefit: prequestions before segments of an educational video improved later test performance on both the prequestioned and the non-prequestioned material [2]. The estimate form plays the part of the prediction and the reveal plays the demonstration, so the page locks each estimate before any ground truth appears.

    Calibration feedback follows the reveal. Hattie and Timperley conclude that feedback is among the strongest influences on learning, and that its type and the way it is given decide whether it helps [3]. In software estimation specifically, lessons-learned sessions on professionals' own estimates did not improve estimation accuracy, while feedback about other professionals' estimation performance produced more realistic uncertainty assessments than the same feedback about one's own [4]. The discussion therefore opens on the class distribution for each task, with every pair's estimate on one axis, before any pair examines its own error.

    The wording of the estimate question draws on the planning fallacy: people underestimate their own task completion times, focusing on plans rather than past experience, while observers overestimate the completion times of others [5]. Asking about an experienced engineer new to the repository puts pairs in the observer's position and matches the ground-truth definitions. The activity also previews the idea behind METR's time horizon, which measures task difficulty by how long humans take [6]; the reveal lets you check whether human time and agent success point the same way on your two tasks.
